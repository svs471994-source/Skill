# Abstract

Salinity limits rice yields on roughly one billion hectares of salt-affected land, and the seedling stage is where the crop is most exposed. This study tested whether six rice endophytes (IS-01 to IS-06) and five dual-strain consortia assembled from these isolates (C1-3, C1-5, C2-3, C2-5, C4-5) keep seedlings of the salt-susceptible cultivar IR-64 growing and metabolically stable under 0, 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl. At 150 mM, salinity cut seedling vigour index (SVI) by 57.7%, chlorophyll *a* by 82.8%, soluble protein by 60.3% and soluble sugars by 67.5%. Inoculation did not change germination significantly (likelihood-ratio χ^{2} = 11.61, df = 6, *p* = 0.071), but the inoculant × NaCl interaction was significant (*p* < 0.001) for every continuous growth and biochemical trait except consortium shoot length. At 150 mM, IS-06 raised chlorophyll *a* by 240%, soluble sugars by 169% and SVI by 142% over the salinity-matched control. Consortium C4-5 (IS-04 + IS-05) kept 80.8% of its unstressed protein, against 39.7% in uninoculated plants, and exceeded both parent strains for protein, chlorophyll *a* and SVI. A single growth–metabolite axis carried 51.7% of the variance in principal component analysis, and a composite membership index ranked IS-06 (0.647) and C4-5 (0.617) first under severe stress. Three pairings (C1-3, C1-5, C2-3) ranked below both parent strains, so the value of a consortium depended on which strains were combined. IS-06 and C4-5 are the strongest candidates for field evaluation.

**Keywords:** bacterial endophytes; microbial consortium; seed bacterization; salinity tolerance; osmolytes; photosynthetic pigments; rice

# Highlights

• Six rice endophytes and five dual-strain consortia were screened under 0 to 150 mM NaCl.

• Salinity reduced seedling vigour by 57.7% and leaf soluble sugars by 67.5% at 150 mM.

• Inoculation did not alter germination significantly but protected growth and leaf metabolites.

• Isolate IS-06 and consortium C4-5 ranked highest on a composite resilience index.

• Consortium benefit depended on the partner pair; three of five pairings underperformed.

# 1. Introduction

Rice feeds more than half of the world's population, yet it is one of the more salt-sensitive cereals, and its sensitivity peaks during seedling establishment and again at flowering (Singh et al., 2021). The problem is large. About one billion hectares of land carry some form of salinity or sodicity (Basak et al., 2022), and irrigated rice systems in South and Southeast Asia sit squarely inside that footprint (Saleem et al., 2025). Breeding has produced tolerant cultivars, but progress is slow because tolerance is controlled by many genes acting at different growth stages (Ismail & Horie, 2017). Interventions that can be deployed within a single cropping season are therefore needed on marginal saline land.

Salt damages plants in two overlapping phases. The first is osmotic: a saline root zone lowers the water potential of the soil solution, so roots struggle to take up water and cell expansion slows within hours. The second is ionic. Sodium and chloride accumulate in older leaves, displace potassium, disturb enzyme function and speed up leaf senescence (Munns & Tester, 2008). Riding on both phases is oxidative stress. When CO_{2} fixation slows, electrons pile up in the chloroplast and reactive oxygen species (ROS) form faster than the antioxidant system can remove them, damaging membranes, pigments and proteins (Gill & Tuteja, 2010). Plants respond by adjusting ion transport and metabolism and by accumulating compatible solutes such as soluble sugars, proline and glycine betaine (van Zelm et al., 2020). Because the size of these adjustments differs between genotypes and treatments, physiological markers like chlorophyll, soluble protein and sugars are widely used to score salt responses (Negrão et al., 2017).

Endophytic bacteria offer a different route to tolerance. They live inside plant tissues for at least part of their life cycle without causing disease (Hardoim et al., 2015). Rice roots, stems, leaves and seeds carry a diverse endophytic community, with *Bacillus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Enterobacter* and *Pantoea* among the genera most often isolated (Santoyo et al., 2016). Because endophytes already occupy host tissues, they are thought to face less competition from the soil microflora than rhizosphere inoculants do. Seed-borne endophytes are particularly interesting. They travel with the seed, colonize the seedling early and can be passed on vertically (Walitang et al., 2017). Their composition in rice shifts with host genotype and with salt stress itself (Walitang et al., 2018), which hints that some of these bacteria have been selected for life in a saline host.

How do these bacteria help? The best-studied mechanism is the enzyme 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase. It breaks down ACC, the immediate precursor of ethylene, and so blunts the burst of stress ethylene that otherwise inhibits root elongation (Glick, 2014). Growth-promoting bacteria also produce indole-3-acetic acid, solubilize phosphate and other nutrients, release siderophores and, in some cases, fix nitrogen. Under salinity, a further set of traits becomes relevant. Inoculated rice plants have shown lower shoot Na^{+}, better K^{+} uptake, higher antioxidant enzyme activity and altered hormone profiles (M. A. Khan et al., 2021; Asif et al., 2023), and inoculated seedlings often accumulate more soluble sugars and other osmolytes. Halotolerant strains carrying ACC deaminase have promoted rice seedling growth under salt, and salt-tolerant bacteria have improved both growth and grain yield of rice in saline soil. Microbe-mediated salt tolerance is therefore best seen as a set of complementary mechanisms rather than a single trait (Ilangumaran & Smith, 2017; Etesami & Glick, 2020).

That view leads naturally to consortia. If no single strain carries every useful trait, combining two or more compatible strains might cover more of the stress response. A meta-analysis of 51 live-soil studies found that consortium inoculation raised plant growth by 48% on average, compared with 29% for single-strain inoculation (Liu et al., 2023). Consortia have also improved wheat growth and yield under saline conditions (M. Y. Khan et al., 2022), and a ten-member synthetic community colonized rice roots together as a stable group (Moronta-Barrios et al., 2018). The case is not closed, though. Strains can compete for the same niche, and poorly matched partners may cancel each other out. Most rice studies still test either a single strain or a single mixture (Ganie et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). Comparisons that line up individual isolates next to consortia built from those same isolates, across germination, seedling growth and leaf biochemistry, are scarce.

A second gap is methodological. Screening papers often report dozens of traits one at a time, each with its own significance test, and rank inoculants by whichever trait looks best. That makes it hard to see whether an inoculant helps the plant as a whole or only moves one number. Standardizing traits and combining them, through indices and multivariate ordination, gives a fairer picture (Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016).

This study was built around those two gaps. The objectives were to (i) assemble dual-strain consortia from six rice endophytes and check their in vitro compatibility; (ii) compare isolates and consortia for effects on germination and seedling vigour in a laboratory assay; (iii) test the same inoculants in a polyhouse pot trial under 0, 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl, measuring growth, photosynthetic pigments, phenolics, soluble protein, soluble sugars and DPPH radical scavenging; and (iv) rank the inoculants with salinity-induced reduction and mitigation indices, a composite membership index and principal component analysis. It was hypothesized that at least one consortium to outperform both of its parent strains under severe salinity, and that the benefit of mixing would depend on which strains were paired.
