# Abstract

Salinity limits rice yields on roughly one billion hectares of salt-affected land, and the seedling stage is where the crop is most exposed. We asked whether six endophytic bacteria recovered from rice (IS-01 to IS-06), and five dual-strain consortia assembled from them (C1-3, C1-5, C2-3, C2-5 and C4-5), could keep salt-stressed seedlings of the salt-susceptible cultivar IR-64 growing and metabolically stable. Seeds were bacterized and tested first on moist filter paper and then in a polyhouse pot trial at 0, 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl. We measured germination, root and shoot length, seedling vigour index (SVI), chlorophyll *a* and *b*, carotenoids, total phenolics, soluble protein, soluble sugars and DPPH radical scavenging. Salinity cut SVI by 57.7%, chlorophyll *a* by 82.8%, soluble protein by 60.3% and soluble sugars by 67.5% at 150 mM. Inoculation did not change germination significantly in either assay (likelihood-ratio χ^{2} = 11.61, df = 6, *p* = 0.071 for isolates), but it strongly affected every continuous growth and biochemical trait (two-way ANOVA, inoculant × NaCl interaction *p* < 0.001 for all traits except consortium shoot length). At 150 mM NaCl, IS-06 raised chlorophyll *a* by 240%, soluble sugars by 169% and SVI by 142% relative to the salinity-matched uninoculated control. Consortium C4-5 (IS-04 + IS-05) kept 80.8% of its unstressed protein level, against 39.7% in uninoculated plants, and exceeded both parent strains for protein, chlorophyll *a* and SVI. Principal component analysis placed 51.7% of the variance on a single growth–metabolite axis, and a composite membership index ranked IS-06 (0.647) and C4-5 (0.617) first under severe stress. Not every pairing helped: C1-3, C1-5 and C2-3 ranked below both of their parent strains. We therefore recommend IS-06 and C4-5 for multi-location field testing, with strain identity and compatibility confirmed before formulation.

**Keywords:** bacterial endophytes; microbial consortium; seed bacterization; salinity tolerance; osmolytes; photosynthetic pigments; antioxidant activity; rice

# Highlights

• Six rice endophytes and five dual-strain consortia were screened under 0 to 150 mM NaCl.

• Salinity reduced seedling vigour by 57.7% and leaf soluble sugars by 67.5% at 150 mM.

• Inoculation did not alter germination significantly but rescued growth and leaf metabolites.

• Isolate IS-06 and consortium C4-5 ranked highest on a composite resilience index.

• Consortium benefit depended on the partner pair; three of five pairings underperformed.

# 1. Introduction

Rice feeds more than half of the world's population, yet it is one of the more salt-sensitive cereals, and that sensitivity peaks during seedling establishment and again at flowering (Singh et al., 2021). The problem is not small. About one billion hectares of land worldwide carry some form of salinity or sodicity (Basak et al., 2022), and irrigated rice systems in South and Southeast Asia sit squarely inside that footprint (Saleem et al., 2025; Sackey et al., 2025). Breeding has produced tolerant cultivars, but progress is slow because tolerance is controlled by many genes acting at different stages (Ismail & Horie, 2017). Farmers on marginal saline land need something they can use this season.

Salt damages plants in two overlapping phases. The first is osmotic: a saline root zone lowers the water potential of the soil solution, so roots struggle to take up water and cell expansion slows within hours (Munns & Tester, 2008). The second phase is ionic. Sodium and chloride accumulate in older leaves, displace potassium, disturb enzyme function and speed up leaf senescence (Munns & Tester, 2008; van Zelm et al., 2020). Riding on both phases is oxidative stress. When CO_{2} fixation slows, electrons pile up in the chloroplast and reactive oxygen species (ROS) form faster than the antioxidant system can remove them (Gill & Tuteja, 2010). Membranes, pigments and proteins are damaged in the process. Plants respond by adjusting signalling, ion transport and metabolism (Zhu, 2016), and by accumulating compatible solutes such as soluble sugars, proline and glycine betaine (Parida & Das, 2005; Mahajan & Tuteja, 2005). The size of these adjustments differs between genotypes, and for that reason physiological markers like chlorophyll, soluble protein and sugars are widely used to score salt responses (Negrão et al., 2017).

Endophytic bacteria offer a different route to tolerance. By definition they live inside plant tissues for at least part of their life cycle without causing disease (Hardoim et al., 2015). Rice roots, stems, leaves and seeds carry a diverse endophytic community, with *Bacillus*, *Pseudomonas*, *Enterobacter* and *Pantoea* among the genera most often isolated (Santoyo et al., 2016; Moronta-Barrios et al., 2018; Afzal et al., 2019). Because they already live inside the host, endophytes are thought to face less competition from the soil microflora than rhizosphere inoculants do (Compant et al., 2019). Seed-borne endophytes are particularly interesting. They travel with the seed, colonize the seedling early and can be passed on vertically (Walitang et al., 2017). Their composition in rice shifts with host genotype and with salt stress itself (Walitang et al., 2018), which hints that some of these bacteria have been selected for life in a saline host.

How do these bacteria help? The best-studied mechanism is the enzyme 1-aminocyclopropane-1-carboxylate (ACC) deaminase. It breaks down ACC, the immediate precursor of ethylene, and so blunts the burst of stress ethylene that otherwise inhibits root elongation (Glick, 2014). Growth-promoting bacteria also produce indole-3-acetic acid, solubilize phosphate and other nutrients, release siderophores and, in some cases, fix nitrogen (Glick, 2012; Etesami & Maheshwari, 2018). Under salinity, a further set of traits becomes relevant. Inoculated rice plants have shown lower shoot Na^{+}, better K^{+} uptake, higher antioxidant enzyme activity and altered hormone profiles (M. A. Khan et al., 2021; Asif et al., 2023; Siddika et al., 2024). Exopolysaccharide-producing endophytes can bind sodium around the roots (Sun et al., 2020), and inoculated seedlings often accumulate more soluble sugars and other osmolytes (Arora et al., 2020; Ramasamy & Mahawar, 2023). Halotolerant *Enterobacter* strains carrying ACC deaminase have promoted rice seedling growth under salt (Sarkar et al., 2018; Sagar et al., 2020), and salt-tolerant rhizobacteria have improved both growth and grain yield of rice in saline soil (Shultana et al., 2020). Several reviews now treat microbe-mediated salt tolerance as a stack of complementary mechanisms rather than a single trait (Ilangumaran & Smith, 2017; Numan et al., 2018; Etesami & Glick, 2020; Zamanzadeh-Nasrabadi et al., 2023).

That view leads naturally to consortia. If no single strain carries every useful trait, combining two or more compatible strains might cover more of the stress response. A meta-analysis of 51 live-soil studies found that consortium inoculation raised plant growth by 48% on average, compared with 29% for single-strain inoculation (Liu et al., 2023). Consortia have also improved wheat growth and yield under saline conditions (M. Y. Khan et al., 2022), and a ten-member synthetic community colonized rice roots together as a stable group (Moronta-Barrios et al., 2018). The case is not closed, though. Strains can compete for the same niche, and poorly matched partners may cancel each other out. Most rice studies still test either a single strain or a single mixture (Ganie et al., 2021; Wang et al., 2025). Comparisons that line up individual isolates next to consortia built from those same isolates, across germination, seedling growth and leaf biochemistry, are scarce.

A second gap is methodological. Screening papers often report dozens of traits one at a time, each with its own significance test, and rank inoculants by whichever trait looks best. That approach makes it hard to see whether an inoculant helps the plant as a whole, or only moves one number. Standardizing traits and combining them, through indices and multivariate ordination, gives a fairer picture (Negrão et al., 2017; Jolliffe & Cadima, 2016).

This study was built around those two gaps. Our objectives were to (i) assemble dual-strain consortia from six rice endophytes and check their in vitro compatibility; (ii) compare isolates and consortia for their effects on germination and seedling vigour in a laboratory assay; (iii) test the same inoculants in a polyhouse pot trial under 0, 50, 100 and 150 mM NaCl, measuring growth, photosynthetic pigments, phenolics, soluble protein, soluble sugars and DPPH radical scavenging; and (iv) rank the inoculants with salinity-induced reduction and mitigation indices, a composite membership index and principal component analysis. We expected at least one consortium to outperform both of its parent strains under severe salinity. We also expected that the benefit of mixing would depend on which strains were paired.
